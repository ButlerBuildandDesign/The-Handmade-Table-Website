Who We Are

Our Story

Four decades of working with pre-industrial reclaimed wood — building furniture, preserving history, and doing it the old way.

The Beginning

Origin Story

Some things have a way of finding you. That's about the best way to explain how David Grant Howard ended up making tables in a small shop in Knoxville, Tennessee. It wasn't where the road seemed to be pointing, not by a long shot — but looking back, it's hard to imagine it going any other way.

David grew up in Greeneville, Tennessee, tucked into the foothills of the Appalachian Mountains on one of the old roads that carried settlers west into Kentucky and beyond. History wasn't something you read about in Greeneville — it was something you walked through every day. Names like Nathaniel Greene, Davy Crockett, Daniel Boone, Andrew Jackson, Sam Houston, and Confederate General John Hunt Morgan weren't just figures in a textbook; they were part of the furniture of the place. Someone once asked David where his love of history and old things came from, and he thought on it a moment before saying, "I don't really know." A close childhood friend standing nearby spoke right up: "David, look around you."

He spent many an afternoon after school at the home of the great-granddaughter of President Andrew Johnson, who gave tours of the former president's house in Greeneville. She had a gift for bringing the past to life, and she left a lasting mark on a curious young boy who was already paying close attention to everything around him.

David was a student athlete at Greeneville High School — lettered in track and basketball, captain of the football team. He went on to the University of Tennessee, where he earned a degree in accounting and finance. After graduation he made his way to Dallas, Texas, spent several years in international banking, and earned his CPA certification. He left banking for the consulting firm of Ernst & Young and lived for stretches in Washington, D.C., Chicago, and Calgary before landing back in Dallas. By any measure, he was well on his way in the corporate world.

Then his youngest sibling bought and dismantled a historic log house.

That was the turn. What started as idle curiosity about an old structure coming apart board by board grew into something he couldn't set down. Before long, David had left the world of spreadsheets and balance sheets behind and walked headlong into a career in historic buildings and antique building materials. He started and eventually ran one of the largest warehouse operations in the country dealing in historic materials — old heart pine, hand-hewn timbers, barn wood, threshing floors, materials that had lived long lives before anyone thought to save them.

It was in that work that he noticed something. His clients were doing beautiful things with historic materials in their homes, but when it came time to furnish those spaces, they couldn't find furniture that matched the quality and character of what was around it. Fine reproduction farm tables and harvest tables — the real kind, made with care and old wood and honest joinery — just weren't out there the way they should have been.

"For whatever reason," David has said, "I was always able to see things others could not see. Using historic materials to make cabinetry was one of them. It just made sense for our customers."

So he started making tables.

Not in any grand or ambitious way at first — just a small enterprise, a response to a clear need, built around the idea that a table ought to be worth something. Worth the wood it's made from. Worth the hands that shaped it. Worth keeping. The farm and harvest tables David began producing were reproductions of the kind of tables that fed families for generations — wide boards running the full length, honest construction, nothing fussy about them. He personally chooses nearly every piece of material that goes into his furniture, with an eye trained by years of handling the real thing.

People often find it amusing that a man with a background in accounting and finance has such a feel for wood. David tends to smile at that.

Today, he works out of a shop in west Knoxville, Tennessee.

The work that comes out of that shop is hard to find elsewhere. Eighteen-foot tables made in three pieces so they can make it through the door of a beach house. Three matching tables cut from the same boards so they nest together perfectly and can be pulled apart again when the holidays are over. Islands married from old antique bases and threshing floor tops. A jelly cupboard with an entire kitchenette tucked inside, looking for all the world like it's always been there.

A lot of what they've made over the years walked out the door before anyone thought to photograph it. That's the nature of honest work — it doesn't always wait around to be admired. But what remains, and what they continue to make, tells the story plainly enough.

Carefully made. The old-fashioned way.

That's The Handmade Table.

Our Philosophy

Reclaimed Wood is Sacred

Every piece of pre-industrial wood in our workshop carries centuries of history. A beam from an 1820s barn, planks from a mill that operated for 150 years, floorboards that have supported generations of families — these materials deserve respect.

Our approach isn't to cover up that history or sand it away. It's to honor it. The patina, the saw marks, the grain — these are features, not flaws. We design around them, highlight them, and let each piece tell its own story.

That's the heart of what we do. Not just building beautiful furniture, but preserving and celebrating the objects that came before.
